The Vault Was Never Locked by One Man

Ten years of Americans trying to pry open the cost of healthcare, 2016–2026

Honed Proof — Checked. Traced. Honed.

Research by Muse · Edited by Freddie Avalos5 sectionsAbout 51 min read

Mark Cuban did not start this fight. He arrived in the middle of it.

When Cost Plus Drugs launched in January 2022 — a pharmacy that posts its math in public: what the drug cost, a 15% markup, a pharmacist's fee, shipping [1] — it looked like a billionaire had single-handedly declared war on American drug prices. The truth this report documents is longer and more interesting. By the time Cuban's pharmacy opened, a radiologist had already founded it, hospitals had already built their own drug factory, a dying patient had already built a national movement, one state employee had already forced every hospital in Montana to accept a set price, and Congress had already passed the law that ended surprise medical bills.

Cuban is the megaphone. This report is about everyone else holding a pry bar — and about the uncomfortable pattern their decade reveals: America passed almost every reform on this list. Getting them to take effect is another story.

How to read this report

Every reform named below carries a status tag. This is deliberate. The decade's central fact is the gap between passed and in force.

  • IN FORCE — operating now, as described.
  • PHASED — law passed; effect arrives on a stated date.
  • ENJOINED / IN COURT — blocked or being litigated.
  • ENACTED, NOT OPERATIONAL — on the books; nothing has actually happened yet.
  • MEASURED — someone counted the result. The number follows.

Claims are graded the Honed way: a figure is Documented when a primary or reputable source stands behind it, and vendor or advocacy numbers are labeled with whose they are. Projections are never printed as results.

01

1. Why Americans overpay: the machine underneath

American healthcare is expensive for structural reasons, and the structures have names.

The chargemaster. Every hospital keeps a list of nominal prices that almost nobody pays — except the uninsured and the out-of-network, who get billed closest to them. The evidence anchor predates our window by months: in June 2015, researchers Ge Bai and Gerard Anderson published the "Extreme Markup" study in Health Affairs, finding 50 hospitals charging roughly ten times their Medicare-allowable costs, against a national average of 3.4 times. Forty-nine of the fifty were for-profit. [2] The chargemaster is not a price list. It is an opening bid in a negotiation patients are not invited to.

Secret negotiated rates. What insurers actually pay hospitals was, until 2021, a trade secret protected by contract. Prices for the same service at the same hospital varied by payer, invisible to the employers funding the plans and the patients paying the deductibles. RAND's employer-led Hospital Price Transparency Study put the standing number on it: in 2018, private plans paid hospitals 247% of Medicare on average for the same services at the same facilities. [3, 4] The study's fifth round restates the trend in medians — 254% in 2018, 253% in 2022. [3] MEASURED.

Facility fees. When a hospital buys a physician practice, the same doctor in the same office can suddenly bill twice: a professional fee, and a "facility fee" for the building. Nothing about the care changed. The ownership did. Facility fees are not a billing quirk; they are a reason hospitals acquire practices, which makes them a consolidation product.

The pharmacy middlemen. Between a drug manufacturer and your pharmacy counter sit pharmacy benefit managers — PBMs — who negotiate rebates, set pharmacy reimbursement, and, in the classic model, keep the difference. Two mechanisms matter:

  • Spread pricing: the PBM charges your plan more than it pays the pharmacy and pockets the gap. Ohio's auditor measured it: in one year of Medicaid data (2017–18), PBMs kept $224.8 million in spread — $208.4 million of it on generic drugs alone, 31.4% of everything the state spent on generics. [5, 6] Kentucky's own report found its PBMs keeping $123.5 million in a single year (2018). [6] MEASURED.
  • Rebate walls: manufacturers pay rebates for preferred formulary placement, which rewards high list prices — a drug with a $500 list price and a $300 rebate beats a $200 drug with no rebate, for everyone except the patient paying a percentage of the $500.

Consolidation and private equity. Hospitals merged; then they bought the doctors. By 2022, 47.9% of primary-care physicians were affiliated with a hospital system, up from 25.2% in 2009 [7], and a January 2025 JAMA Health Forum study of 226 million negotiated prices found office visits priced 10.7% higher at hospital-affiliated practices and 7.8% higher at private-equity-affiliated ones [7] than at independent practices. By January 2026, one industry census counted 82% of physicians employed by hospitals or corporate entities. [8] The specialties private equity rolled up first — emergency medicine, anesthesiology, radiology — will reappear in Section 3, dominating the federal arbitration docket. That is not a coincidence. It is the business model, twice.

02

2. The people who pushed back

The founder behind the famous pharmacy

Cost Plus Drugs was founded by Alex Oshmyansky, a radiologist who cold-emailed Mark Cuban in 2018 with the pitch. [1] His first attempt, a nonprofit, failed to raise money; the company was re-formed as a public-benefit corporation, with Cuban as co-founder, backer, and — by his own design — megaphone. The model is real and it works, within limits this report will state plainly in a moment: posted prices, cost plus 15%, no rebates, no spread. IN FORCE since January 2022.

The hospitals that built their own drug company

Civica Rx (founded 2018) is what happens when the buyers become the manufacturer. Seven health systems and three philanthropies created a nonprofit generic-drug company run like a utility: long-term purchase contracts, one transparent price to all members, a six-month buffer stock against shortages. [9] Its consumer arm, CivicaScript, priced its first outpatient drug roughly $3,000 a month below the average Medicare Part D cost. [10] Its insulin — manufactured by Biocon while Civica's Virginia plant awaits commercial production — reaches pharmacies at $45, with a maximum retail price of $55 for five pens. [11]

California then did something no state had done: it became a drug label. CalRx, the state's partnership with Civica, sells those insulin pens at a maximum of $11 per pen from January 1, 2026, against cash prices CalRx cites above $500 for a five-pack. [12] IN FORCE. In August 2026 the same label began supplying free albuterol inhalers to roughly 10,000 public schools. [13] A state, acting as a buyer with a brand, got a price no regulator had managed to impose.

The patient, and the money behind the evidence

David Mitchell was a multiple myeloma patient whose own drugs cost about $440,000 a year, by STAT's account, when, in 2016, he founded Patients For Affordable Drugs NOW [15] — the only national patient organization devoted solely to lowering drug prices, refusing pharmaceutical industry funding on principle. He died in January 2026 [14], having lived to see Medicare negotiation — the policy his organization spent a decade building the case for — take effect.

The case was substantially financed by philanthropy. Civica was co-founded by three philanthropies — the Laura and John Arnold Foundation, the Peterson Center on Healthcare, and the Gary and Mary West Foundation — at $10 million each. [9] Mitchell's organization drew early backing from the Arnold Foundation, and Arnold Ventures (Laura and John Arnold) funded a roughly decade-long, ~$100 million drug-pricing research portfolio of its own [16], a portfolio inspired in part by Peter Bach of Memorial Sloan Kettering's Drug Pricing Lab — who with Leonard Saltz had made news in 2012 by refusing an overpriced cancer drug and watching its maker offer hospitals a 50% discount. [17] And Antonio Ciaccia of 46Brooklyn Research became the states' PBM auditor-for-hire, helping Ohio surface its $224.8 million spread [5]; his signature exhibit is a PBM billing $2,000 to $8,000 a month for a cancer drug available generically for about $30. [18]

Fixing the plan from inside

Cash-pay pharmacies route around insurance. The transparent PBMs — Navitus, Capital Rx, SmithRx, Liviniti, AffirmedRx, MedOne, Rightway — rebuild the benefit inside it: a flat administrative fee, 100% of rebates passed through, no spread. In August 2023, Paul Markovich of Blue Shield of California proved a major insurer could do it, dropping CVS Caremark as primary PBM after fifteen years and unbundling the job among five vendors — though CVS Caremark remained one of the five, for specialty pharmacy. [19] Blue Shield projects savings of up to $500 million a year. [19] That figure is a projection, not a result, and this report prints it as one.

The ceiling on cost-plus — stated plainly

Cost-plus pricing works on commodity generics, where several manufacturers compete. It has no leverage on a patent-protected brand drug with one legal maker, which is why Cost Plus's brand catalog is thin and it does not sell insulin. Two studies bracket the consumer math:

  • JAMA Health Forum (2024), examining some 844 million fills: Cost Plus would have been cheaper on only 5.5% of Medicare fills and 7.1% of private-insurance fills — but 28.9% of fills by the uninsured. [20]
  • Annals of Internal Medicine (2026), matching 62 million commercial claims: once a patient's copay or coinsurance passed $15, the cash price won roughly 77–80% of the time. [21]

And the hidden friction: cash purchases generally do not count toward your deductible or Medicare Part D out-of-pocket threshold. The honest rule: if your copay is a low flat fee, insurance usually wins; if you are uninsured, in a deductible, or facing percentage coinsurance, check the cash price every time. That check is what Honed Proof's Prescription Price Finder exists to help you run.

A caution this report owes you: not every cost-plus lookalike earned its listing. DiRx, a direct-to-consumer pharmacy frequently recommended alongside Cost Plus, drew sustained customer reports through 2024–2026 of weeks-long processing, backorders, and unshipped orders. We name it here only to flag it: a model is not a recommendation, and a cheap price on a drug that never arrives is not cheap.

The people who posted prices, and the people who graded them

The Surgery Center of Oklahoma, co-founded by G. Keith Smith, posted all-inclusive surgical prices online in 2009 [22] — surgeon, facility, anesthesia, one number — years before any law required it, and co-founded the Free Market Medical Association in 2014 [23] to match transparent sellers with self-funded employers. David Goldhill's Sesame Care (2018) built a cash-pay marketplace of listed, upfront prices across all fifty states. [24] Jeanne Pinder's ClearHealthCosts did journalism as price research — her teams documented the same blood test at $19 in one New Orleans lab and $522 a half-block away. [25]

When federal rules finally forced hospitals to publish prices in 2021, Cynthia Fisher's PatientRightsAdvocate.org graded the homework. The series is the decade's transparency report card: 5.6% of hospitals fully compliant six months in; 24.5% in the 2022–23 review; [26] 49.4% in September 2026 [27] — the highest recorded, and still only 18% of hospitals posting actual dollar-and-cents prices for at least half their services. [28] The trajectory was not monotonic: PRA's own series ran 36.0% in July 2023, 34.5% in February 2024, and 21.1% in November 2024 between the second and third of those grades. [26] MEASURED. Compliance is converging. Usable prices are not. Turquoise Health (Chris Severn and Adam Geitgey, founded 2020) [29] built the business of cleaning those disclosure files into something searchable — increasingly for the industry itself.

The bookshelf, and the research that became law

Three authors, three separate books — not a team, despite frequent grouping: Elisabeth Rosenthal's An American Sickness (2017), Marty Makary's The Price We Pay (2019), and Marshall Allen's Never Pay the First Bill (2021), a how-to manual for fighting bills line by line. [30] Allen died in May 2024 [31]; the work continues through the academy and project bearing his name. Dan Weissmann's podcast An Arm and a Leg (2018– ) has chronicled the ground level throughout: patients fighting bills, and what actually worked.

Makary's own arc belongs in the record: the exposer became the regulator, serving as FDA Commissioner from April 2025 to May 2026. [32]

The direct line from research to law runs through Zack Cooper and Fiona Scott Morton, whose 2016 study of surprise out-of-network bills — roughly one in five emergency visits produced one [33] — was taken up by lawmakers directly, with Loren Adler translating the payment-dispute designs. The result was the No Surprises Act. Its sequel is in Section 3, and it is not a happy one.

The failure that proves the thesis: Haven

In January 2018, Amazon, Berkshire Hathaway, and JPMorgan Chase announced Haven, a nonprofit venture to fix healthcare for their combined 1.2 million employees, prescription drugs explicitly included. Surgeon and author Atul Gawande was hired to run it. In February 2021, Haven shut down, with 57 employees. [34]

The post-mortems converge: vague goals; a visionary CEO who was not an operator; each founder quietly building its own competing healthcare ventures; and, decisively, no local market power — 1.2 million employees scattered across the country could not dominate a single hospital market, so Haven could not force a single price down. [35] Three of the richest companies in America learned what Section 1 says: in healthcare, prices are set market by market, by whoever holds local share. Contrast Civica, CalRx, and Cost Plus — each picked one lock and shipped a priced product.

3. Washington: sunlight, surprise bills, and the long PBM fight

Mandating sunlight

The federal transparency era began with the first Trump administration: an executive order in June 2019 [36], a hospital price-transparency rule finalized that November and effective January 1, 2021 [37], and a Transparency in Coverage rule requiring insurers to publish negotiated rates from July 2022. [38] IN FORCE — with Fisher's grades (Section 2) as the standing measure of what "in force" was worth in the early years.

In 2026, both files were rebuilt. The hospital rule (effective January 1, 2026, enforcement from April 1) replaced estimated allowed amounts with actuals computed from remittance data — median, 10th-percentile, and 90th-percentile amounts, with a named official attesting to accuracy. [39] In October 2026, HHS Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. and CMS Administrator Mehmet Oz finalized the Transparency in Coverage overhaul: one file per network, a single data format, executive attestation, phased through 2027–28. [38] Honesty requires the other half: the same rule loosened some duties, moving reporting from monthly to quarterly and allowing "ghost rates" to be dropped. [38] PHASED.

Congress, meanwhile, tried to write the rules into statute so no administration could unwind them. The Patients Deserve Price Tags Act (Senators Marshall and Hickenlooper) and the Lower Costs, More Transparency Act of 2026 (Representatives Guthrie and Pallone) both cleared committee in July 2026. [40, 41] As of November 2026, neither is law.

The surprise-bill law, and its expensive sequel

The No Surprises Act (passed December 2020, effective January 1, 2022) [42] was built by a bipartisan Senate working group led by Bill Cassidy with Maggie Hassan, Michael Bennet, and others [43], merged with a House bill from Frank Pallone and Greg Walden. It ended most surprise out-of-network bills for patients. IN FORCE. What it built for the insurers and providers, though, was an arbitration system — federal Independent Dispute Resolution — and the arbitration system became a story of its own:

  • Regulators expected tens of thousands of disputes a year. They got 2.56 million in 2025 alone [44] and roughly 7.8 million cumulatively by September 2026. [45]
  • Three initiating parties — HaloMD (an arbitration-filing intermediary), TeamHealth, and SCP Health, the latter two private-equity-backed staffing firms in exactly the specialties Section 1 flagged — filed about 38% of disputes in late 2025. The top ten filed about two-thirds. [44]
  • Providers won about 85% of determinations in 2025 [44], at a median award of 445% of the benchmark rate when they prevailed — Georgetown CHIR's figure, from its analysis of CMS data. [47] Payments above in-network rates totaled $15.6 billion across 2023–2025; [46] the EBRI research institute sizes 2025 awards at about $46–50 per person [48] with employer coverage — $46 across all covered lives, $50 for those under 65 — — real premium pressure, honestly sized at well under 1% of plan costs, not an explosion.
  • The courts kept reshaping it: in August 2026, the Fifth Circuit, sitting en banc, vacated central parts of the benchmark-calculation rules in Texas Medical Association v. HHS. [49]
  • The agencies' June 2026 operations rule — published June 4, 2026 and effective August 3, 2026 — cut the administrative fee from $115 to $15 for disputes initiated on or after June 11, 2026, raised the batching cap from 25 to 50 line items for disputes whose open negotiation begins on or after November 1, 2026, and mandated standardized remittance codes for services furnished on or after January 1, 2027. [50, 51] By September 2026, closures were finally outpacing new filings. [52]

The patient protection worked. The payment machinery behind it became a revenue line for the very staffing model the law was meant to discipline. Both sentences are true, and any honest account has to print both.

Taking on pharma and the PBMs

The Grassley–Wyden insulin investigation (Senate Finance, report January 14, 2021) reviewed more than 100,000 pages of internal documents [53] and documented the rebate machine from inside: one major insulin's list price rose 27% over five years while its net price fell 10%; [54] Sanofi's rebates to CVS Caremark clients climbed from 2–4% of list in 2013 to as high as 56% in 2018; [55] manufacturers privately weighed cutting list prices roughly in half in 2018 and declined [54], because smaller rebates meant worse formulary placement.

Oversight had a theatrical arm: Representative Katie Porter's whiteboard hearings — Celgene's Revlimid, $215 to $719 a pill, with $500,000 of its CEO's bonus tied to the increases (2020); [56] AbbVie's roughly $50 billion in buybacks and dividends put against its research claims (2021). [57] And Senator Bernie Sanders, chairing the HELP Committee, ran an investigation through the first half of 2024 that extracted $35 inhaler caps from three of the four major makers — Boehringer Ingelheim, AstraZeneca, and GSK [59] — and brought Novo Nordisk's CEO to testify in September 2024. [58] Teva did not commit. That exception is part of the record, and this report keeps it in.

The decade's largest federal intervention was the Inflation Reduction Act (2022): Medicare, for the first time, negotiated prices directly. The IRA’s $35 Medicare insulin cap began in 2023; [60] its first ten negotiated prices took effect January 1, 2026 [61], with inflation-rebate provisions also operating on their own earlier statutory schedule. A second round — 15 drugs, including semaglutide at a negotiated $274 against a $959 list price [62] — takes effect January 1, 2027, and a third round, the first to reach Part B drugs, in 2028. [63] PHASED. Manufacturers sued to stop the program; on May 18, 2026, the Supreme Court declined to hear their challenges [64], leaving the lower-court rulings upholding negotiation in place. The legal fight is, for now, over.

Running alongside the statute, the executive branch cut its own deals: by January 2026, sixteen manufacturers had signed most-favored-nation pricing agreements. [65] One number deserves scrutiny — Novo Nordisk's deal promised Medicare semaglutide at $245 [65], below the $274 price the government's own negotiators had just won under the IRA. Whether executive deal-making strengthens or quietly undercuts statutory negotiation is an open question this report flags rather than answers.

Federal PBM reform arrived last and latest. Standalone PBM bills — spread-pricing bans, rebate pass-through mandates — advanced through committees for years without becoming law. What finally enacted was folded into the Consolidated Appropriations Act of 2026 (signed February 3, 2026): for Medicare Part D, PBM pay is delinked from drug list prices and converted to flat fees from 2028, with "any willing pharmacy" network rules from 2029. [66] PHASED. The state playbook of Section 4, adopted federally — on a federal timeline.

4. The states: fifty laboratories, one recurring result

The states passed almost everything. The honest headline of the state decade is how little of it has taken effect — and the exceptions prove what makes reform real: a buyer, a benchmark, and a start date nobody could litigate away.

Make it yourself vs. import it

California's CalRx (Section 2) chose manufacturing-by-contract. Florida and Colorado chose importation from Canada under federal Section 804 — and produced the decade's cleanest contrast between authorization and operation. The FDA authorized Florida's program in January 2024 and Colorado's in June 2026. [67, 68] As of November 2026, neither state has imported a single drug. Florida has paid its logistics vendor roughly $81.9 million and taken five extensions; [69, 68] Colorado approached 23 manufacturers and, as of December 2023, none had agreed to supply it. [70] By June 2026, after FDA authorization, Governor Polis was saying 10 manufacturers had agreed to get on board. [135] ENACTED / AUTHORIZED, NOT OPERATIONAL — both of them.

Price caps meet the courts

Eleven states enacted prescription drug affordability boards; five gave theirs authority to set upper payment limits [71] (some trackers count four, omitting Oregon). Only two states have actually set one:

  • Colorado set the nation's first cap in October 2025: Enbrel at $600 per weekly dose, roughly $31,000 a year [72] against the ~$53,000 Colorado plans had paid. [73] In July 2026, a federal judge enjoined it before it ever took effect, on patent-preemption grounds. [72] ENJOINED / IN COURT.
  • Maryland, the first board state (2019), moved slower and narrower: caps on Jardiance ($204 per 30 days) and Ozempic ($274 — pegged to Medicare's negotiated price) for state and local government purchasers only, with final regulations approved November 16, 2026, voluntary from 2027 and mandatory for participating purchasers from 2028. [74] A proposed cap on a third drug, Farxiga, was dropped after generic competition arrived [75] — the market did that one itself. PHASED.

The attrition is part of the story: New Hampshire dissolved its board in 2025, Ohio defunded its own, and Virginia's governor vetoed a board bill for the third time in May 2026. [76] One industry tally counts $28 million spent standing boards up across six states [77] before a single cap took effect anywhere — a contested figure from an interested source, printed here with that label because the underlying fact pattern (money spent, caps not yet operative) is documented.

The PBM crackdowns

All fifty states enacted some PBM law in the decade — licensure, anti-gag, anti-spread rules are now the national floor. Two states tried to go further and break up vertical integration itself:

  • Arkansas Act 624 (2025), the first law barring a PBM from owning pharmacies, was preliminarily enjoined within months, on dormant Commerce Clause and federal preemption grounds; the Eighth Circuit heard argument in September 2026. [78] ENJOINED / IN COURT.
  • Tennessee's FAIR Rx Act, signed May 22, 2026, was drafted to dodge Arkansas's defects [79] and targets companies owning a pharmacy, a PBM, and an insurer at once. Divestiture is due July 1, 2028; three federal lawsuits arrived within weeks; CVS has warned its 134 Tennessee stores could close. [79] PHASED / IN COURT.

The quieter layer kept working underneath: California's SB 41 (2025) bans spread pricing and mandates full rebate pass-through from 2026; [80] Illinois's Prescription Drug Affordability Act did the same from January 1, 2026; [81] Louisiana banned spread pricing outright in 2025, with enforcement effective January 1, 2027; [82] Kentucky consolidated Medicaid pharmacy under a single PBM and, by the state's own report, recorded $0 spread across its managed-care plans in fiscal 2025. [83] MEASURED — by the state itself, and labeled so.

And one state result deserves its own line, because it reframes a popular policy: 29 states and DC now cap insulin copays [84], and New York went furthest — insulin cost-sharing is $0 in state-regulated plans from January 1, 2025. [85] Utah studied its $30 cap and published the honest verdict: median patient out-of-pocket cost fell 58.5%, plan costs rose 22%, total cost unchanged. [86] MEASURED. Caps move who pays. They do not move the price. Every insulin cap in America should be read with Utah's sentence attached.

Purchaser power: the reforms that measured out

The strongest state results came not from regulating prices but from setting them as a buyer — reference pricing, paying hospitals a multiple of Medicare instead of a discount off the chargemaster:

  • Montana (2016): Marilyn Bartlett rebuilt the state employee plan at an average of 234% of Medicare; [88] every hospital signed; the plan saved $47.8 million in three years with no premium increases. [87] MEASURED. The model's run ended with a 2023 change of plan administrator [87] — a final accounting, not a going concern, and a warning about how person-dependent these wins are.
  • Oregon (2019–20): capped its public-employee plans at 200% of Medicare in-network, 185% out. [89] $107.5 million saved in 27 months, member out-of-pocket costs down 9.5% on outpatient procedures [89, 90], hospital finances stable in follow-up study. MEASURED.
  • North Carolina ran the same play and lost: Treasurer Dale Folwell's Clear Pricing Project (2018–19) offered hospitals up to ~196% of Medicare for 727,000 covered lives; the eight major systems boycotted together, only five hospitals signed [91], and Folwell backed down — the State Health Plan's actuary projected $486 million in added cost over the following three years, while the General Assembly's actuary put it at $534 million. [92] The story's actual ending is compromise: in 2024 all 99 eligible hospitals joined a Medicaid-linked debt-relief program (more than $6.5 billion in relief, state-reported) [93], and Folwell's successor negotiated a tiered preferred network including the same systems that boycotted, from 2027. [94]

The roster keeps growing: Washington's 200% cap for state-employee plans starts January 1, 2027; [95] New Mexico capped its state-employee plan in 2025; [96] Vermont's Act 68 (2025) goes furthest, phasing Medicare-based reference pricing into the commercial market from October 2026 [96] — the boldest state price-setting experiment since Colorado's cap, and an inevitable court test. Delaware created a hospital cost review board in 2024 [97] and followed it with 2026 legislation capping prices at 250% of Medicare by 2033 [96]; a lawsuit settlement trimmed the board's budget-veto power, not the cap. [97] Reform by settlement is still reform, but it is a different animal.

Exposure infrastructure — and its built-in hole

Twenty-five states have a mandatory all-payer claims database operating or in implementation. [98] The consumer-facing ones are genuinely useful — New Hampshire's NH HealthCost, Colorado's Shop for Care, Maine's CompareMaine, Massachusetts' CompareCare. [99] Texas built the model twice in one year (2021): SB 1137's hospital price-posting law with penalties up to $1,000 a day, and HB 2090's claims database [100, 101] — which the state's own biennial report concedes was never fully funded [102], running on its host university's support while it asked the legislature for an appropriation. Alaska wrote the purest exposure statute in the country: providers post their most common services, facility fees stated separately, in the reception area, in type at least 20 points high. [103]

Every one of these systems has the same hole, cut by the Supreme Court in Gobeille v. Liberty Mutual (2016): [104] states cannot compel self-funded employer plans — the plans covering most working-age Americans — to submit their claims. The database is most blind exactly where most people are covered.

On the narrower fronts: roughly ten states now restrict or expose facility fees in some form (Connecticut and Colorado lead; Ohio bars them only for hospital-owned primary-care practices, and only from January 1, 2028). [105] Right to Shop programs — cash rewards for choosing lower-cost in-network care — began with New Hampshire and Kentucky's state plans [108] (Kentucky's SmartShopper has run since 2015, an administrative program rather than a statute [106]) and reached statute in Oklahoma in 2022, in voluntary form. [107] And fifteen states plus DC now bar medical debt from credit reports — Colorado first, in 2023 [109] — a protection already in court, with the federal consumer bureau arguing in 2025 that federal law preempts the state bans, and Colorado's own ban set to sunset in 2028. [110, 109] IN FORCE / IN COURT, both, simultaneously.

The territories: a different problem entirely

The territories do not have a price-transparency problem. They have a funding-architecture problem. Territorial Medicaid runs on fixed annual federal allotments under Section 1108 [111, 112] — not the states' open-ended entitlement — at enhanced matching rates (83% for most territories, 76% for Puerto Rico) [111] that stop mattering the moment the cap is exhausted; after that, the territory pays 100%. Puerto Rico's enhanced match and cap expire after fiscal 2027 without congressional action. [113] American Samoa runs Medicaid under the Section 1902(j) waiver Congress enacted for it in 1982 [112, 114], and the Northern Mariana Islands also operates under Section 1902(j) [112] — American Samoa makes no individual eligibility determinations at all; federal funds flow to its single hospital system. [115] Medicare drug coverage does reach the territories — through Medicare Advantage drug plans and stand-alone Part D plans in Puerto Rico and American Samoa — but territorial residents are shut out of the federal Extra Help subsidy; instead, the Enhanced Allotment Plan (§1935(e)) sends the territories allotments to fund drug coverage for low-income residents. [116, 117, 112]

Puerto Rico's own attempts tell the story in miniature: its 2019 PBM law is frozen by the federal PROMESA oversight board; [118, 119] its Medicaid pharmacy contract makes one vendor, Abarca Health, both PBM and rebate aggregator; [120] and its most significant pricing development of 2026 was joining — as one of 41 signatories — the federal GENEROUS model [121], which negotiates Medicaid drug prices toward most-favored-nation levels. The Territories Health Equity Act, which would end the caps, was introduced in December 2025. [122] It has not passed. ENACTED, NOT OPERATIONAL does not even describe the territories; for them, the operative phrase is authorized, not funded.

5. What remains unresolved

The ERISA shield. Roughly two-thirds of Americans with employer coverage are in self-funded plans [123], and federal law places those plans largely beyond state reach: state transparency mandates, affordability-board caps, balance-billing rules, and claims-database reporting mostly stop at the self-funded line. The Supreme Court has carved one exception — Rutledge v. PCMA (2020) lets states regulate the PBMs that serve those plans [124] — and one confirmation: in 2026 the Sixth Circuit held in McKee Foods that ERISA preempts a state PBM anti-steering law. [125] Every state victory in Section 4 carries this asterisk. It is the single largest structural fact in American health policy, and the least discussed.

Transparency is not reduction. A decade of disclosure moved shoppable care — planned procedures, imaging, labs — where patients can compare and choose. It has not disciplined emergency care, where no one shops, or consolidated markets, where there is nothing to choose between. RAND's standing number is the evidence: after a decade of transparency laws, private plans still paid hospitals 254% of Medicare (the fifth round's headline figure; 253% as a median). [3] Publishing a price is not the same act as lowering it, and the states that measured real reductions — Montana, Oregon — did it by setting a price as a buyer, not by posting one.

Enacted is not in force. Read Section 4 again as a list of statuses: the first-ever price cap, enjoined before its start date. The ownership ban, enjoined within months. Two importation programs, authorized for years, zero drugs imported. A claims database its own state never funded. A divestiture law whose deadline is 2028 and whose courtroom fight began in 2026. A debt-protection ban that sunsets in 2028 while a federal agency argues it was preempted all along. This is not an argument that reform failed. It is the report's central finding: in American healthcare, passage is the midpoint of a reform's life, not the end of it — and any coverage that stops at the signing ceremony is telling you half the story.

The arbitration paradox. The No Surprises Act protected patients, completely and genuinely. It also built an arbitration market that processed 7.8 million disputes [45], paid out $15.6 billion above in-network rates across 2023–2025 [46], and concentrated its volume in private-equity-backed staffing firms and filing intermediaries — one of which insurers have sued repeatedly, without success. A protection for patients became a pricing lever for the protected-against. Whether the 2026 operations overhaul and the courts' reshaping of the benchmark tame it is the live question of 2027.

Caps shift costs; they rarely cut them. Utah's insulin verdict generalizes: a copay cap is a decision about who pays, and a good one — patients facing $440 insulin bills needed relief, and got it. But the price itself survives every cap aimed at the patient's share of it. The same caution applies to affordability-board caps, whose savings accrue to the government purchasers they bind, with no legal duty to pass a dollar to patients, and to the 340B drug-discount program — now the second-largest federal drug program [127], its purchases up from $6.6 billion in 2010 to $43.9 billion in 2021 (CBO) [134], and ~$66 billion in 2023 [126] — where the law never required the discount to reach the patient at all, where industry-funded studies blame it for billions in employer premium costs, and where the states' attempts to police its pharmacy networks produced, in 2026, a full circuit split: a Fourth Circuit panel held West Virginia's and Maryland's contract-pharmacy laws preempted while the Fifth and Eighth Circuits upheld similar laws — but the full Fourth Circuit has since taken the cases en banc, vacating the panel decisions (argument October 27, 2026; decision pending). [129] 340B now has a cost argument and a preemption war running at once.

The territories' unfinished architecture. Fifty states argue about prices inside an open-ended federal financing system. Five territories argue about survival inside a capped one, with Puerto Rico's funding cliff arriving after fiscal 2027. Until Congress treats territorial Medicaid as an entitlement rather than an allotment, no pricing reform available to a governor in San Juan, Hagåtña, or Pago Pago touches the actual problem.

The three kinds of reform — a closing frame

Everything in this report does one of three things. It cuts a price (negotiation, caps, reference pricing, manufacturing it yourself). It exposes a price (transparency rules, claims databases, posted cash prices). Or it shields a patient from a price (surprise-bill protection, copay caps, charity care, debt-reporting bans). The third kind can be fully successful while prices rise untouched — Dollar For, which enforces hospitals' existing charity-care duties and reports eliminating $55 million in patient bills in a single year [130], changes no price in America, and matters enormously. The second kind is necessary and insufficient. The first kind is the rarest, the most litigated, and — Montana, Oregon, Medicare negotiation — the only kind with measured price reductions attached.

Know which kind you are looking at, and half the confusion in American healthcare coverage dissolves.

What you can do with this today

You cannot repeal the chargemaster before your next prescription. You can refuse to pay its logic at the pharmacy counter:

  1. Price the same drug four ways — your copay, a discount card (GoodRx or SingleCare), a cash-pay pharmacy (Cost Plus, Costco, Amazon Pharmacy's RxPass if you have Prime), and the pharmacy's own cash price. Same drug, same dose, same quantity. Honed Proof's Prescription Price Finder walks through exactly this comparison.
  2. Remember the $15 rule of thumb from the 2026 Annals study: if your copay or coinsurance is above ~$15 [21], cash is worth checking; below it, insurance usually wins — and cash purchases generally don't count toward your deductible.
  3. If a hospital bill arrives, ask for the financial-assistance policy before you pay anything. Nonprofit hospitals are legally required to have one; organizations like Dollar For exist because eligible patients routinely never hear that.
  4. If you get a surprise out-of-network bill for emergency or hospital-based care, the No Surprises Act is on your side [42] — the dispute is between the insurer and the provider, and your cost-sharing is capped at in-network levels. That part of the system works. Use it.

The vault door in this report's cover image took a decade of hands to move at all. It moved. The honest summary of 2016–2026 is not that the system was beaten — it is that the system was, for the first time, priced in public, and that everything since has been an argument about whether a public price is the beginning of a lower one.

Method and sources note

This report was researched across primary sources — CMS rules and data releases, state statutes and board records, court decisions, state auditor reports, and peer-reviewed studies (RAND; Health Affairs; JAMA Health Forum; Annals of Internal Medicine; EBRI) — and cross-checked against multiple independent research passes, with every conflicting figure adjudicated to its primary source before use. Where a number belongs to an interested party — a vendor's savings claim, an advocacy organization's tally, a state's report on its own program — the text says so. Figures Freddie's Prescription Price Finder uses are reader-entered prices, not live lookups; the Finder ranks what you type in, which is precisely the gap the claims databases cannot fill.
