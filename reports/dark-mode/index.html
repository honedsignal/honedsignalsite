Verdict: Nuanced — saturated as a look in some categories, not oversaturated on the web

Is dark mode oversaturated?

Your iPhone and Mac live in dark mode, and it feels like every website followed them there. Five years of adoption data, usability research, and trend reporting say the feeling is half right: devices went dark — websites mostly didn’t. Dark is the uniform in the rooms a tech and finance brand competes in, and still a minority choice on the web as a whole.

Confidence is high for the measurements within the large website corpora examined, and medium for category-level saturation, which relies on smaller curated samples and trend commentary. Throughout, Data marks measurements and peer-reviewed or NN/g research; Opinion / Trend marks designer commentary — useful signals, not evidence.

01

Three findings drive the verdict

1

Users’ devices went dark; websites mostly did not

Self-selected enthusiast polls report high dark-mode use — 81.9% said they “use dark mode” in 2020 and 73.3% said “always on” in 2026 — but their samples and question wording differ, so they are not population estimates or directly comparable measurements. NN/g’s smaller, less explicitly enthusiast-focused mobile survey splits users roughly into thirds — dark, light, and mixed. Yet only 12.2% of measured homepages render dark by default, and only 12% of sites used prefers-color-scheme at all in 2024. On the open web, dark-first is still a minority choice. Data

2

Inside tech, finance, and media, dark is the category uniform

Gaming homepages are 45% dark-by-default and entertainment 32% in the same 158k-site corpus; developer tools, crypto and finance dashboards, streaming, and AI-tool marketing cluster dark; a curated sample leaning toward startups and dev tools measured 37.4% dark defaults. In those rooms, dark navy no longer differentiates — it signals category membership. That suggests a positioning issue rather than, by itself, a usability problem. Data + Trend

3

The usability evidence punishes forcing either mode

For normal-vision readers, light mode wins measured reading performance; dark wins subjective comfort in low light and genuinely helps some low-vision users. NN/g’s conclusion is not “pick light” or “pick dark” — it is mirror the user’s OS setting by default and let them override. In NN/g’s usability sessions, participants thought about dark mode at OS level and barely noticed when a site didn’t support it; that is a finding from those sessions, not a population estimate, and NN/g itself notes the limits of its testing environment. Users are very unlikely to abandon a site over it. Data

Bottom line for a small dark-navy brand (Honed Signal’s #0B0E14 + orange + teal): keep the dark identity where it earns its keep — tools, dashboards, media, hero artwork — stop forcing it where it costs readability — long-form reports — and ship a proper light theme driven by prefers-color-scheme with a persistent manual override, defaulting to light when no preference is available. Differentiation should come from accent discipline, typography, and artwork, not from abandoning a palette users in this category demonstrably like. The toggle at the top of this page is that recommendation, working.

02

Adoption: devices went dark, websites didn’t

Handle the user numbers with care — the samples differ wildly, and there is no high-quality, representative, multi-year series for OS dark-mode share, because Apple and Google do not publish one. The defensible read: one-third in the one less-enthusiast survey, rising higher among younger and enthusiast samples — and higher still at night. The platforms reviewed here still ship light as the out-of-box default (Windows 11 explicitly so) — dark is opt-in or scheduled for most of the population. Data

03

Timeline: novelty to mainstream option — for operating systems

The “novelty → mainstream option” story is true for OSes, apps, and user expectation. It is not true for websites as shipped artifacts. Foundations (2018–2020) are included because the five-year window starts mid-story.

Sep 2018–2019

Foundations. macOS Mojave ships system Dark Mode; Safari 12.1 (2019) adds prefers-color-scheme for web content. Apple’s HIG and Google’s Material dark theme codify the rules: Material’s surface is #121212, not pure black, with white text at 87% opacity and desaturated accents.

Sep–Oct 2019

The mainstreaming event. iOS 13 (system dark, with scheduling) and Android 10 (system dark theme) launch within weeks of each other.

Early 2020

prefers-color-scheme works everywhere by early 2020. Supported in all major browsers — a site can finally follow the OS with pure CSS.

9 Sep 2021

Google Search on desktop gets a dark theme (Light / Dark / Device default) — arguably the highest-traffic page on earth normalizes the toggle. Data

5 Oct 2021

Windows 11 releases with matched light and dark themes — but ships with light as the default. Dark is a user choice; the platforms reviewed here follow this pattern.

2021 → 2024

The web lags. Sites using prefers-color-scheme: 7% (2021) → 8% (2022) → 12% (2024), per the HTTP Archive Web Almanac.

27 Aug 2023

NN/g publishes its dark-mode user research: “popular, but not always a critical feature.” Users think at OS level; the prescription is to mirror the system setting.

2024–2025

The counter-aesthetic becomes visible: warm-cream editorial exemplars (Anthropic/Claude) are widely documented and imitated; Pantone names warm brown Mocha Mousse Color of the Year 2025. Opinion / Trend

Dec 2025

Android 16 QPR2 introduces an opt-in Expanded Dark Theme that can automatically darken eligible apps without native dark-theme support. It is another reason to provide a properly designed native theme rather than relying on forced inversion. Data

2026

Pantone names Cloud Dancer (soft off-white) Color of the Year 2026 — the first white-family pick in the program’s history, per Pantone coverage. And the corpus lands: 12.2% of homepages dark by default; only 5.4% of measured pages include a dark-mode toggle. Five years after Google Search went dark, within the measured corpus, the web’s center of gravity is still light.

04

The three starting-point videos — checked

The framing finding: none of the three videos is actually about dark mode, dark-mode saturation, or a dark-vs-light trend. All three are color-palette tutorials. Treating them as evidence about oversaturation would be a category error — their real value here is Payton Clark Smith’s color-association claims and the 60-30-10 proportion rule, which a two-accent palette is at risk of breaking. All three were verified live on YouTube on 18 Nov 2026. Data

Video 1 — “The Science of Website Colors | A Complete Color Guide”

Payton Clark Smith (132K subscribers) · 28 Jul 2023 · 8:04 · 28,280 views when checked

Argument Opinion — each color “represents” fixed traits and suits named industries: blue = trust, loyalty, reliability — best for software, finance/banking, healthcare, and his personal favorite; orange = warmth, friendliness — strong for calls-to-action; white = clean, peaceful, “basically the default… it looks good in every single situation” — “I absolutely love white websites”; black = power, tech, seriousness, luxury — usable almost anywhere but can read as too serious. He also warns that overusing colors destroys contrast and readability.

Verification: the fixed hue→emotion dictionary is overstated. The peer-reviewed review (Elliot & Maier, Annual Review of Psychology, 2014) finds color effects are context-dependent; Labrecque & Milne (2012) support only narrower, context-bound associations. His white/black remarks, however, align with this report’s data: light remains the web’s actual default, and dark connotes “tech” precisely because tech categories converged on it.

Could not fully verify: the transcript line at ~7:45 about black websites was truncated in YouTube’s transcript panel; the sense is “very popular/trending right now,” but the exact wording could not be confirmed — so it is not quoted verbatim anywhere in this report.

Video 2 — “60-30-10 Color Rule”

Jesse Showalter (445K subscribers) · 4 Mar 2022 · ~6 min · 2,168,349 views when checked

Argument Opinion / practice — in mobile UI, color looks “mature and seamless” rather than “obnoxious and loud” when applied roughly 60% dominant/neutral, 30% secondary/brand, 10% accent/CTA. He explicitly calls it “more like a guideline” and closes with breaking the rules. Nothing in the video is about dark vs light.

Verification: 60-30-10 is a long-standing interior-design heuristic imported into UI; there is no peer-reviewed evidence for the exact percentages — it is practitioner convention, and Showalter frames it that way himself. Its defensible core is scarcity: an accent works because it is rare. Applied to a dark-navy brand: a navy 60% base fits the rule; two equally vivid accents (orange and teal) competing at CTA level are exactly what it warns against.

Video 3 — “How To Pick The Perfect Website Color Palette | Beginner’s Guide”

Paige Brunton Business (23.9K subscribers) · 13 Oct 2025 · 13:46 · 16,007 views when checked

Argument Opinion / practice — sites look “off/amateur” when beginners break three things: (1) color harmonies — complementary (e.g. blue/orange: high contrast, good for CTAs), analogous, split-complementary, monochromatic; (2) “color seasons” — palette temperature picked from brand words; (3) 60-30-10. Her bad example runs colors at ~33/33/33 — “all shouting for attention equally,” with unreadable text; her good example fixes contrast and proportions.

Verification: same status as Video 2 on 60-30-10 (heuristic, not science), and her “seasons” framework has no empirical literature behind it. Her contrast point is the one claim here backed by hard requirements. The example combines competing accents with insufficient text contrast; these are separate design issues — WCAG AA requires 4.5:1 for normal text. Her showcased palettes skew light/warm — consistent with, but not evidence for, the 2024–2026 editorial counter-trend.

Net assessment: useful for how to proportion and discipline a palette — directly applicable to navy/orange/teal. Not usable as evidence that dark mode is oversaturated — none of them claims that, measures that, or discusses OS dark mode at all. Any argument built on them for switching to light would be misattribution.

05

Preference vs performance: what the studies say

Users say they use dark for eye strain, battery, and looks (NN/g, 2023). They perform slightly better in light for reading. They feel better in dark at night. All three can be true at once — which is precisely why the evidence converges on choice plus system-matching, not a single imposed mode. Data

Reading: light wins on performance

The positive-polarity advantage: Piepenbrock et al. (Düsseldorf; Ergonomics 2013 / Human Factors 2014) found dark-on-light beat light-on-dark on visual-acuity and proofreading tasks for both younger (18–33) and older (60–85) adults — and the advantage grows as font size shrinks. A brighter field constricts the pupil, reducing aberrations. Participants did not perceive the difference: performance and preference diverge. NN/g (Budiu, 2020): “in users with normal vision, light mode leads to better performance most of the time” — while still recommending designers allow switching.

Dobres et al. (MIT AgeLab) found no significant polarity difference for glanceable reading in simulated daylight — and light performed better at simulated night, especially at small sizes.

Eye strain: comfort, performance, and health are different questions

Three things are often conflated here. Subjective comfort: the 2026 study (Nguyen et al.) found statistically significant differences in participants’ self-reported eye strain depending on ambient lighting and display mode — dark can genuinely feel easier in dim environments. Objective performance: that same study found no statistically significant difference in reading speed or comprehension, consistent with the wider literature — an immersive-VR study (Erickson et al. 2020, cited by NN/g 2023) found dark slightly better only when the whole environment was dim, and the advantage over a dimmed light mode was very small; brightness and ambient light matter as much as polarity. Long-term eye health: no lasting benefit or harm from either mode is established. So dark mode can improve perceived comfort in dim environments — without establishing that it universally reduces eye strain or improves reading performance.

Who dark genuinely helps

In Legge et al.’s small 1985 study, 4 of 7 participants with cloudy ocular media (e.g. cataracts) read faster with white text on black; the other 3 showed no consistent polarity advantage. Some low-vision and photophobia users strongly prefer dark. NN/g’s accessibility bottom line: “for people with visual impairments, it’s best not to enforce either display mode” — respect the system choice.

Astigmatism and halation

Bright text on near-black can bloom (“halation”) for people with astigmatism. An older U.S. population estimate puts clinically significant astigmatism — at least 1.0 diopter — at roughly 1 in 3 adults; that is not “any degree” of astigmatism. Halation itself is a plausible optical mechanism — a dark field dilates the pupil and exposes more optical imperfection — rather than an effect that prevalence research directly demonstrated in dark-mode interfaces. Mitigations are well established: avoid pure white (#FFF) on pure black (#000); use off-white text on dark gray (Material’s #121212 pattern); avoid both extremes in dark: thin weights get swallowed, while heavy weights bloom because light-on-dark renders bolder.

Long-form reading specifically

The performance literature (small-text proofreading) plus the 2026 preference data — only 45% prefer dark for sustained reading, vs 82% for video — point the same way: dark is the weaker default for long text, the stronger default for media and low-light sessions. That single asymmetry drives the surface-by-surface verdict in §07.

OLED battery savings: real, conditional, usually small

Power saved switching light → dark — Purdue University (Dash & Hu, MobiSys 2021), six Google apps on OLED phones

Solid = low end of the range, pale = up to the high end. OLED/AMOLED only (about half of smartphones produced were OLED as of 2023, per NN/g); on LCD laptops and desktops the saving is ~zero, and media-heavy pages save least because photos and video are identical in both modes. NN/g cites Purdue figures of 67% at 100% / 14% at 30% (see §09). At everyday brightness the figures point to single-digit-to-low-teens savings, not the headline number. Battery is a minor argument for offering dark on mobile — never for dark-only design.

06

The counter-trend — and where dark remains particularly prevalent

Since 2023 a swing toward light, warm, editorial design is genuinely visible — but read the labels: most of this section is Opinion / Trend, and what is not happening matters just as much. None of the platform examples reviewed here has deprecated dark mode, and support expectations keep rising (Android 16 QPR2’s Expanded Dark Theme is opt-in, and darkens eligible apps that never built a native theme). The counter-trend is about defaults and brand surfaces, not dark disappearing.

Editorial as explicit differentiation

2026 trend analyses describe a bifurcation: “techno-futurist” (dark + neon + shaders + bento grids) vs an editorial counter-aesthetic (cream + serif + warm + restrained) adopted because the category converged. Anthropic’s warm-cream, serif-led site is the canonical example — “when a category converges on a single visual grammar, the most strategic move becomes refusing the convergence” — alongside Notion’s warmth and PostHog’s hand-drawn personality (Toimi, Web Design Trends 2026). Design extractions document Claude/Anthropic’s canvas as warm cream (#FAF9F5 / #F8F8F6) with a single clay-orange accent (#D97757 / #CD6F47): a research brand, in the AI category, deliberately not dark.

“Light” now means warmed near-white

Norrly’s 2026 trends report — based on its own extraction from “hundreds of award-level/top sites,” vendor-run with method undisclosed in detail, so treat as directional — states the majority of top sites ship light by default, and that light now means warmed near-white (#FAF8F4–#FDFDFB), rarely pure #FFFFFF, with dark retained as “a strong genre choice for developer tools, crypto, and studios” and best practice = offering both. This page’s light theme is built on exactly that #FAF8F4 family.

Color-of-the-year signals

Pantone: Mocha Mousse (warm brown) for 2025, then Cloud Dancer (soft off-white) for 2026 — the institutional color world is signaling warm/light, not neon-on-black. WGSN/Coloro’s 2026 pick, Transformative Teal, is a saturated mid-tone — notably, teal survives as an accent, not a base. Trend reports that simultaneously list “dark mode as system default” as a 2026 must-ship (light-only “looks dated”) and “warm light editorial” as the fresh look are not contradicting themselves: the emerging norm is dual-mode with a light-leaning brand face in content and consumer categories.

Where dark remains particularly prevalent

Media/streaming — dark-only by design (the theater logic). Gaming — 45% dark-by-default homepages, the highest category share measured. Developer tools / IDEs / terminals — dark is the entrenched professional default; surveys consistently report large dark-theme majorities, though the exact 70–91% figures circulating come from secondary/blog citations and could not be verified to a primary survey table — direction solid, numbers soft. Finance dashboards / trading / crypto — dark terminals (Bloomberg heritage), data-dense screens, long monitored sessions. Night-first products — sleep, astronomy, automotive, home-theater control.

The differentiation trap, both ways. Dark alone no longer distinguishes in tech/finance — dark navy + glowing accent is now the category’s entry uniform, and the “Inter-on-black” AI-startup look is a recognized cliché. But switching to light solely to be different trades one uniform for another increasingly familiar aesthetic: cream + serif is already becoming the 2025–26 counter-uniform, Anthropic imitators included. Distinctiveness was never carried by background luminance — it is carried by typography, accent discipline, illustration, layout rhythm, and voice. Switching light-only also carries real risks: expectation mismatch for dark-OS users (NN/g describes users being “subjected to the blast of white screens”), night glare, a harder trust signal in finance/dev categories where dark connotes “serious terminal-grade tool,” and the print/PDF reality that long-form research gets printed, screenshotted, and re-rendered by email clients in both polarities regardless.

07

The verdict, surface by surface

One brand, three different answers — because the evidence differs by what the page is for. Dual-mode is not free (it is a second design system: surfaces, dividers, shadows, graphics, and charts all need re-checking), so scope it to the surfaces users live in longest. Forced single-mode is defensible only where the mode is the product logic.

Long-form research reports

System-adaptive, light fallback.
Dark available, never forced.

The positive-polarity advantage is largest for small text and proofreading (Piepenbrock); only 45% prefer dark for sustained reading (2026 survey); light prints, annotates, and embeds cleanly. Research reports should follow the reader’s system preference, offer a persistent manual override, and default to light when no preference is available. Print and PDF versions should use the light theme. Offer the dark theme for night readers (long sessions are one of NN/g’s key cases for supporting dark mode). Body text ≥16–17px with generous line-height; off-black ink on warm off-white rather than pure #000 on #FFF.

Calculators, tools & dashboards

System-adaptive dual-mode,
dark fully designed — not inverted.

This is the strongest case for keeping dark prominent: long, frequent, often evening sessions in a dark-norm category (finance tools), where users expect OS mirroring (NN/g). Charts and data-viz must be re-authored per mode — gridlines, series colors, saturated-on-dark vibration. If only one mode can ship first, ship the one matching the category and the audience — for Honed Signal, dark — with light fast-following.

Media / video-led pages

Dark-first — dark-only is justified.

Theater logic (the Spotify/Netflix precedent, NN/g): the dark UI recedes and thumbnails and artwork carry the color; 82% prefer dark for video (2026 survey). The battery saving is smallest here, so the argument is purely presentational — and it holds. Keep surrounding text blocks short, and link out to system-adaptive reading pages (light fallback) for long text.

Dark-first vs light-first vs dual-mode

The honest trade-off table — including the cost column most trend pieces skip.

Dark-first (single mode)

Best for: streaming, gaming, night tools, art-led heroes

Pros

  • Category fit for tech / finance / media
  • Artwork and data-viz glow; best night comfort
  • Small OLED battery bonus
  • Still distinctive vs the general web (only 12% dark-default)

Cons

  • Weakest for long-form reading; halation risk for astigmatic users
  • Now the category uniform in tech/finance — low distinctiveness there
  • Graphics and email breakage risk

Light-first (single mode)

Best for: reports, docs, editorial, print-adjacent brands

Pros

  • Best measured reading performance
  • Prints and PDFs natively
  • Warm-editorial freshness, 2024–26
  • Safest for long-form trust with general audiences

Cons

  • Category-signal mismatch in finance / dev / crypto
  • Night glare for dark-OS users
  • Cream-editorial is itself becoming a uniform
  • Forfeits the media / theater effect

Dual-mode — recommended

Best for: any brand with both reading matter and tools — including this one

Pros

  • Matches OS expectation (NN/g best practice)
  • Serves both reading and night use; does not force users into a mode that conflicts with their needs (accessibility)
  • Future-proof vs platform darkening (Android’s opt-in Expanded Dark Theme)

Cons

  • Additional design and QA work across both themes; two palettes to keep on-brand
  • Chart and asset duplication
  • A neglected, broken-feeling second mode is worse than none

Accessibility requirements for dark themes (WCAG 2.2). Same ratios in both modes: AA requires ≥4.5:1 for normal text, ≥3:1 for large text (18pt, or 14pt bold) and ≥3:1 for UI components and meaningful graphics; AAA — the right target for a research brand’s body text — is 7:1 / 4.5:1. Passing the ratio is necessary, not sufficient: pure white on pure black scores 21:1 yet maximizes halation. White on Honed Signal’s #0B0E14 computes to roughly 19:1 — comfortably AAA — but keep body text slightly off-white to soften bloom. Test accents as text: saturated orange and especially teal/cyan on near-black can vibrate and blur (NN/g’s Coinbase example passed at 4.59:1 and still blurred), so any accent used for links, labels, or chart text must individually clear 4.5:1 on the base and on every elevated surface — or be restricted to large/bold and non-text roles, lightened a step for dark mode. Build elevation with progressively lighter surfaces (shadows don’t read in dark), avoid thin body weights, and re-test focus indicators, placeholders, disabled states, transparent graphics, and QR codes in both modes — never invert a scannable code.

08

Recommendations for Honed Signal

For a small brand deciding whether to keep a dark navy palette — #0B0E14 + orange + teal. Short version: the color was never the problem.

1

Keep the navy. Change the contract, not the color.

Move from “dark-only” to “dark identity, system-adaptive delivery”: prefers-color-scheme default plus a persistent manual override. This follows NN/g’s single clearest prescription and addresses a central concern about forcing a single display mode.

2

Split the site by surface

Reports system-adaptive, with light as the fallback; calculators and dashboards dual-mode; media-led pages dark-first — per §07. Research reports follow the reader’s system preference, offer a persistent manual override, and default to light when no preference is available; print and PDF versions use the light theme. Do not let the homepage’s art direction dictate the reading experience of a 6,000-word report.

3

Fix the accent economy — the real lesson of the three videos

Apply 60-30-10 honestly: ~60% navy and neutrals, ~30% secondary surfaces and text tones, ~10% accent total — pick one hero accent. Orange = the action/CTA accent (warm, complementary to navy-blue, per the scarcity logic in Videos 2 and 3); teal = data/semantic accent only (charts, positive data series, code) — never both at full saturation competing for the same click. This, not a background flip, is what will make the brand look less generic.

Build the light theme as “warm paper,” not inverted dark

5

Run a dark-theme accessibility pass before any rebrand debate

Verify every text/accent pair on #0B0E14 and on each elevated surface against WCAG AA 4.5:1 (AAA 7:1 for report body text); switch body text to off-white; check teal-on-navy and orange-on-navy specifically for the vibration NN/g documents; test with an astigmatic reader if possible. The contrast checker in §05 runs the brand pairs live. Begin with a scoped accessibility audit before committing to a redesign.

6

Differentiate on the assets dark can’t commoditize

A recognizable illustration and artwork system, a distinctive serif or grotesque for headlines, and consistent data-viz styling. Corpus data says backgrounds are mostly neutral everywhere — nobody owns a background color; people remember type, art, and voice.

7

Measure your own audience before revisiting this in a year

Log the share of visitors arriving with prefers-color-scheme: dark (a one-line analytics dimension), toggle usage, and report-page completion by mode. Replace the enthusiast-poll guesses in this report with your own denominator — if, say, 70%+ of your readers arrive dark, weight the dark report theme accordingly; the framework in §07 already accommodates either answer.

Note on brand values: The brand hexes used throughout (#FF6B35, #2EC4B6) are the locked Honed Signal brand values; individual accent ratios were computed in the §05 checker rather than in a separate audit. Rec. 5 still requires checking them on every elevated surface, not just the base.

09

What could not be verified

Stated plainly, because a verdict is only as good as its gaps.

  • No representative population statistic for OS dark-mode share exists in public sources — Apple and Google do not publish it. All high percentages come from self-selected enthusiast polls with differing samples and question wording (for example, 81.9% “use dark mode” in 2020 and 73.3% “always on” in 2026); they are not population estimates or directly comparable measurements. The smaller, less enthusiast-focused source (NN/g, n=115) suggests ~⅓. Any report quoting a single global “% of users in dark mode” should be distrusted.
  • No quantitative Awwwards/Dribbble dark-share dataset was found; showcase-saturation claims are qualitative. The Livery (n=409) and ClickFoundry (n=158,294) corpora are the best available proxies, and they disagree in level (37.4% vs 12.2% dark-default) because their samples differ.
  • Video 1, ~7:45: the exact wording of the claim that black websites are currently popular/trending could not be confirmed from the truncated transcript line; substance reported, verbatim quote withheld.
  • Developer dark-theme percentages (70–91% figures for IDEs/Stack Overflow) circulate via secondary blogs; no primary survey table was located. Direction accepted, numbers flagged soft.
  • Purdue battery figures are reported two ways (39–47% at full brightness and 3–9% at 30–50% in Purdue’s own release, vs 67% at 100% and 14% at 30% as cited by NN/g). The cited sources report different savings figures; until their measurement bases are reconciled, those figures are not directly comparable. The qualitative conclusion — small at normal brightness, OLED-only — is consistent across both.
