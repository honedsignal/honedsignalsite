Exactly 8 Arms — Can AI Promise It?

Which art tool can an AI agent actually drive — and can any of them promise an exact limb count? The short answer splits in two: plenty of tools can be driven. None of the generators can make the promise.

No No generator can promise it

The Verdict

No generative image vendor can promise exactly 8 arms — or any exact limb or object count. None claims to in its documentation, and none contractually guarantees anatomical correctness. On controlled counting benchmarks, flagship models get the exact object count right roughly one time in three.

The only way to promise exactly 8 is to stop asking a generative model to count. Build the character as a puppet rig — an SVG vector puppet, a Spine or Live2D 2D rig, or a Blender 3D armature — with exactly eight arm parts. The count becomes a property of the file, countable before rendering, and no render can invent a ninth arm.

We also ran the test ourselves, on our own mascot: twenty poses at 35% yield (~40% once the production rules were applied), then a paid LoRA training run that scored 0/10 on the same briefs — with exhibits.

Two more findings frame the chart. Scaling does not fix it: a 2025 Microsoft Research Asia / Peking University study found counting does not improve when scaling from 2B to 12B parameters and 1K to 500K training samples.[2] And 8 is already a hard number: benchmark performance is documented to degrade beyond five objects, with even state-of-the-art models struggling to exceed 50% accuracy on single-digit prompts — for ordinary objects, before adding the harder problem that arms must also be attached correctly to one body.[2]

Why the models can’t count

The failure is architectural, data-driven and encoding-driven. It is not a prompting bug, and “try a bigger model” is empirically not a fix.

1. There is no counting step in the architecture

Counting requires keeping “a sense of separate identity for every instance… and then carry[ing] out a global computation implicitly during generation. It is still unknown if such representations exist” in diffusion models. The CountGen authors add that models “struggle with controlling spatial layout just from text” and that a correct count “requires obeying a global and complex spatial relation” — while denoising is a local, iterative refinement process with no slot, register, or tally for instances.[1]

The numerosity study states the mechanism plainly: models “lack effective mechanisms to bind textual numerals to spatial instance slots,” and in practice they “primarily optimize with respect to the noise prior, rather than the text instructions, making object count a weak signal easily overridden by stochastic noise patterns” — different noise seeds carry their own preferred counts.[2]

2. Training data teaches “a hand is present,” not “five fingers”

The AbHuman / HumanRefiner authors attribute limb anomalies to training that “typically focuses on aligning overall characteristics, like the human having hands and legs, while less attention is paid to detailed characteristics such as the number of fingers or hands,” leaving models with “limited control over the fine-grained generation of detailed body parts.”[4] Hands compound this: small in frame, frequently occluded, folded or cropped in web photos, highly articulated, and self-similar — so the training distribution rarely presents a clean, labeled, countable hand. In short: the model sees pixels, never bones. There is no skeleton in the model to check the output against.

3. The numeral is a soft signal, not an instruction

Prompts are encoded into embeddings where a numeral is a token associated with scenes that tended to contain about that many things — not an integer executed by a counting routine. The numerosity paper explicitly links the image failure to the LLM “strawberry test” lineage of tokenization / architecture counting failures. Autoregressive / transformer image models change the sampler, not this fact: the same paper lists FLUX, Ideogram V2, GPT-4o image, Nano Banana and Qwen-Image together as struggling “to produce the correct number of objects specified in text prompts” on GeckoNum.[2]

4. The human-figure evidence is worse than the object evidence

  • Distortion-5K (2025): “Nearly 50% of generated images contain distortions” — proliferated limbs, missing fingers, deformed / fused parts — across four popular text-to-image models on a 500-prompt benchmark.[3]
  • AbHuman / HumanRefiner (2024): a failure benchmark built from 56K synthesized human images with 147K annotated anomalies in 18 categories — even SOTA generators (SDXL, Midjourney, DALL·E 3 named) produce limb abnormalities routinely enough to build it.[4]
  • HumanBench (WACV 2026): 36K images across Midjourney, Stable Diffusion, FLUX and three more models — “persistent failures, including disfigurements, species leakage, and counting errors”; two heads, missing legs, misplaced limbs; prompt–image consistency “declines monotonically” with prompt complexity.[6]
  • Kamali et al. (CHI 2025): a human detection study of 450 diffusion images and 149 real images with 50,444 participants and 749,828 observations, establishing a 5-class artifact taxonomy (anatomical, stylistic, functional, physics-violation, sociocultural). The paper itself (arXiv:2502.11989v1, verified against the primary source on 13 Nov 2026) reports anatomical implausibilities at 51.4%, functional implausibilities at 58.7%, and stylistic implausibilities at 39.0% of annotated images.[5]
  • NumBench (2026 preprint — treat with caution, very recent): 640,000 prompts, counts 1–100, 5 commercial + 2 open systems: “performance declines sharply with requested count; all evaluated methods are weak above 50 objects.”[40]

How widespread the problem is

Counting fingers became the public shorthand for “that’s AI” — the first check taught in spotting-AI coverage — precisely because extra and fused digits were the most legible, repeatable error. The CHI 2025 study was motivated by exactly this public circulation.[5]

  • Hands and fingers (the canonical case): six or seven fingers, fused fingers, proliferated or missing hands — quantified at ~50% distortion rates in controlled human-generation benchmarks, not just anecdotes.[3][4]
  • Whole limbs and heads: HumanBench’s title is itself a catalog — “Two Heads, No Legs, But Mostly Human.” HumanRefiner likewise opens with SDXL’s “incorrect limb counts, twisted hands, and incorrect limb position.”[6][4]
  • Horses’ legs: a widely shared equestrian-community post dissects an AI horse-anatomy diagram with extra legs, three hips, backward joints and an invented anatomical label, run as a recurring “What’s Wrong Wednesday” feature; a separate photographer’s AI-transformed horse image drew community criticism for distorted, extra legs and unnatural musculature / gait.[21][22] Production help docs agree anatomical inaccuracy “can be an issue when generating people or animals.”[19]
  • Furniture, bicycles, everyday objects: artifact-repair guides catalog a chair with five legs, an oval bicycle wheel, cups at torso scale, floating / intersecting objects — the same “presence without correct quantity / geometry” failure in inanimate subjects, fixed only by masking and regenerating the whole object.[20]
  • Children’s books (a named production blocker): a 24-page book requires the same character 24 times; creator guides describe page-1 perfection followed by a different face structure (p.3), different hair (p.8), different proportions (p.15) — and publish a pre-flight checklist whose first item is “Finger count: Are there exactly 5 fingers per hand? (AI loves extra fingers).”[17] A companion guide calls character consistency “the single most common frustration in this space,” attributing it to models having no memory between generations.[18]
  • Comics, brand mascots, illustrators: the same two blockers recur — identity drift panel-to-panel, and limb / finger errors that survive into print, where they cannot be patched by re-rolling a feed. That is why the workarounds in the techniques section exist as a cottage industry, and why community negative prompts are litanies of the failure: “extra limbs, bad anatomy, malformed hands, extra fingers, fused fingers.”

Ground News framing

No Ground News story page specifically about extra fingers / AI hands was found in this run (a site:ground.news search returned no results, 13 Nov 2026 — see open questions). What Ground News is, methodologically, is documented by the Columbia Journalism Review: it aggregates 40,000+ outlets and presents each story as a bias distribution (left / center / right percentages) with factuality ratings, under the tagline “See every side of every news story.”[23] Used correctly for this topic, that framing supports one claim and no more: spotting-AI / extra-fingers coverage is not a partisan or single-outlet narrative — it runs as consumer how-to, fact-checking, tech and culture coverage across the spectrum, consistent with a technical failure any outlet’s audience can reproduce in minutes.

Methods — the trace rule

An image passes only if it shows exactly 8 arms and every visible arm segment can be traced continuously to an emergence from the body or to a tip. A segment ending against another arm, a prop, or a frame edge counts as a failure — a “chopped arm” — never as depth. Counts were performed by Muse (Huxley — the editor’s Muse assistant, and the octopus who is the test subject); the editor’s count governs any dispute.

Three production rules were adopted mid-test, on the editor’s direction. The clipboard floats: Huxley’s clipboard is a hologram panel that hovers beside him, touched by zero arms; a prop gets at most one arm, never two — arms spent holding props appear to fall out of the model’s loose arm budget and get backfilled elsewhere. Flat-row language: every later brief specified “one single flat row like a paper cutout, NO arms behind the body, NO back row, wide triangular gaps between the legs, body held HIGH” — with that language, the hidden back-row extras disappeared entirely and failures moved to the front row, where they are visible at a glance.

Count-correct with the LoRA: 2/10 — Welcoming and Proud, both flagged. None of Exercise 2’s four clean passes survived training: Sleeping, Idea, Surprised and Curious all failed with the LoRA. Both of the LoRA’s count-correct results had themselves failed untrained.

  • What training did do: identity. All ten images are unmistakably the same character — headphones, spots, eyes — with no reference image supplied at generation time. The LoRA is a genuine identity lock. It is not a counting mechanism.
  • Behavior and style followed the training data and the base model, not the brief. No training image contained a clipboard, so when the brief mentioned one, the model drew the generic behavior — the character holding a solid clipboard (Exhibit 3) — overriding the production’s floating-hologram rule. And FLUX’s glossy 3D-render house style dominated the output despite the storybook passes in the training set.
  • The failure direction flipped. The untrained generator over-counts (adds rows and gestures). FLUX + LoRA under-counts by merging more often than it over-counts (five under-count rows against three over-count rows) — three failures at exactly 6 arms and one at 6–7 — plus one chopped arm segment ending flat in open water (Exhibit 2).

Bottom line from our own bench: training is an identity tool, not a counting tool — the same conclusion the literature reaches, verified here at first hand for $2.35.

Audited against our own test — same day, same character, same sequence

The workflow the graphic recommends is the exact sequence this production ran on 13 November 2026:

  1. Reference conditioning with the locked character sheet and explicit arm maps: 7 clean passes in 20 poses (35% overall; ~40% per roll once the rules were applied) — an improvement, not reliability.
  2. An approved pose library: the 7 verified passes — Shrug, Dancing, Conducting, Sleeping, Idea, Surprised, Curious.
  3. LoRA fine-tuning on 21 images (14 locked sheet views + those 7 verified passes), $2.00 training, the same ten briefs re-run for ~$0.35: 0/10 clean passes, 2/10 count-correct — both flagged. All four poses that had passed untrained failed trained. Identity lock succeeded; counting did not.

Verdict: no disagreement on the workflow order — references first, library, train last is sensible, and matches this production’s practice. The disagreement is the promise. The graphic’s “reliably… exactly eight arms every time” is the claim the $2.35 test above disproves: training teaches what Huxley looks like — appearance is learnable. It does not install a counter, so the arm count stays a dice roll. Training is an identity tool, not a counting tool. The graphic’s own artwork disproves it too — per the editor’s trace check in the caption above, 0 of its 9 images of Huxley verify at exactly 8.

Limits and confounds — read before citing

  1. Two variables changed at once. The exercises used one hosted generator; the LoRA test used FLUX.1 [dev] via fal.ai — the only hosted trainable route available. This is a comparison of pipelines, not a same-model LoRA on/off ablation, which was not run.
  2. Training size. 1,000 steps is the trainer’s default. Black Forest Labs guidance suggests 1,500–3,000 steps for character work (a $3–6 run at fal’s linear pricing). Untested here; the mechanism — a LoRA encodes appearance, not a countable constraint — gives no reason to expect a count guarantee at higher steps.
  3. One seed per brief per condition. Yields are point observations, not rates with confidence intervals. The direction of the result — no improvement; baseline passes became failures — is the finding, not the exact percentages.
  4. Counts are the production’s own QA, performed under the trace rule above, not an independent benchmark.

What we did with the result

Because of this test, Huxley’s comic character is being built as a deterministic vector puppet — eight arms by construction, arms as separate shapes in the file. Generated art is used for scenes and one-offs only, behind the trace-rule check. That is the architecture recommended later in this report, adopted here first: generative for concepts and backgrounds, rig for the character who has to be right every time.

The one thing that can promise it

A puppet rig carries the count in its structure. Below is the idea in miniature: an SVG puppet whose arms are eight named groups in the file. The button does what an agent does before every render — it counts the arm groups in the source and asserts the total.

arm groups in file: — (press the button to run the pre-render check)

Posing moves arms; it cannot create or delete them. If the rig file contains exactly 8 arm bones / parts, every pose, frame and render contains exactly 8 arms — and the agent can prove it by enumerating the arm nodes before rendering: 8 <g id="arm-*"> groups in SVG, 8 arm objects in Blender’s bpy.data, 8 slots in Spine JSON.

The same logic covers deterministic compositing, with one caveat: approve one arm asset once, then place exactly eight copies as separate layers / groups in Photoshop, Illustrator or SVG and flatten. The count is by construction — but the agent must still render and visually verify all eight are distinct, not fully occluded behind the body or each other. Structure is guaranteed; legibility still needs the QA render.

Notes on the decisive cases: ComfyUI is the best-in-class generative stack for control — its server docs were verified live on 13 Nov 2026: submitting a workflow is POST /prompt, which “validates the prompt and adds it to an execution queue, returning either a prompt_id… or error,” with /history/{prompt_id}, /queue, /ws, /upload/image and /upload/mask — a complete agent loop, and the only stack here that natively composes ControlNet + IP-Adapter + LoRA + inpainting + seed in one graph.[9] Midjourney is the opposite pole: no official public API, and its terms prohibit automated access — every third-party “Midjourney API” is an unofficial wrapper automating accounts in breach of those terms, with ban risk.[12][13] Recraft matters beyond drivability: its vector models return editable SVG, which is inspectable and editable by the agent as data — the bridge to the deterministic promise.[11]

Model IDs churn Hosted model IDs retire fast — in this research run, gpt-image-1 was already listed in OpenAI docs as shut down 23 Oct 2026, and secondary guides reported Imagen 4 Vertex / Gemini endpoints as discontinued in mid-2026 with migration to Gemini Flash Image models. The API patterns are stable; the model ID is not. Pin a current model ID at build time, never hard-code one.

Techniques, ranked: nudge → repair → guarantee

The dividing line is absolute: anything that ends in a diffusion / transformer sampling step can fail the count; anything whose final render is a deterministic function of a counted scene description cannot.

No promise

Prompting & negative prompts

“Exactly 8 arms”, “no extra limbs” — a soft embedding nudge, and the weakest lever. The benchmarks above are measured with the count in the prompt.

No promise

Seed + generate-and-select

Reproducibility ≠ correctness: the same seed and prompt reproduce the same wrong count reliably. Selection helps only if a correct sample happens to occur and a checker catches it. Expected tries at p≈0.3 is ~3–4 per keeper; at p≈0.05 (hard pose / count) ~20 — with no upper bound.

No promise

Reference conditioning — IP-Adapter, character reference

Materially improves who the character is across images (Ideogram / Midjourney character reference, Firefly Custom Models). It conditions appearance, not cardinality — the reference’s arms are not transferred as a counted skeleton.

No promise

Character LoRA / fine-tune

The best generative route to a recognizable, repeatable character. A LoRA trained on an 8-armed character raises the prior for 8 arms — and can still render 7 or 9 on a novel pose, because sampling remains stochastic. First-party check: our own FLUX LoRA, trained on the character’s locked sheet and verified passes for $2.00, scored 0/10 clean on briefs the untrained generator passed 4/10 — identity locked, count did not (evidence and exhibits).

No promise

Region prompting / layout-to-image

An LLM or user supplies one box per instance; attention is confined per box. CountGen’s review: predefined layout “helps ensure” count, but LLM-layout baselines alone scored poorly in its tests (Reason-Out-Your-Layout 15–26%). Two-stage LLM-layout systems report object recall rising 57.2% → 99.9%[42] for the layout stage in a narrow table-setting domain — layout recall, not final-pixel limb accuracy. The renderer can still merge, duplicate or drop a boxed instance.

No promise

ControlNet pose / skeleton / depth / canny — the strongest generative control

Inject a spatial condition — e.g. an OpenPose skeleton drawn with exactly 8 arms — into every denoising step.[7] The peer-reviewed anchor is HumanRefiner’s pose-reversible refinement: 2.9× limb quality vs SDXL, 1.4× vs DALL·E 3 — large relative gains from a low base.[4] Why still no promise: ControlNet is a conditioning strength, not a constraint solver — the model may ignore, fuse or add a limb. And an 8-arm skeleton is far outside OpenPose’s human training distribution (2 arms), so expect the model to “correct” toward 2 arms or tangle arms unless the checkpoint / LoRA also knows the species. Custom skeleton + character LoRA + ControlNet is the best generative attempt — and it still requires verification per frame.

Repair

Inpainting / masking

The standard production repair: “inpaint the hand, not the picture.” Each inpaint is itself a new sampling roll for that region — no promise per operation. It becomes a guarantee only as part of a verified pipeline: inpaint → count-check → inpaint until the checker passes, then stop touching the image.

Verified, not promised

Generate-and-verify loop (agent + detector / VLM counter)

CountLoop formalizes this for objects — LLM layout planner + critic feedback loop — reporting “counting accuracy of as much as 98%” on its high-instance benchmarks.[8] The agent may truthfully say “this delivered file was machine-counted at 8” — never “this tool generates 8.” A QA claim about checked files, not a capability claim about the model.

Promise — with QA caveat

Deterministic compositing (Photoshop / Illustrator / SVG layers)

Count = number of arm layers / groups in the source file, enumerable programmatically before export. Recraft’s native SVG route helps: generated vector art arrives as editable paths / groups the agent can split, duplicate ×8, name arm-1…arm-8, and audit.[11] Caveat: render and visually verify all 8 are distinct in the final frame.

Promise — unconditional

Vector / 3D puppet rigs (SVG puppet, Spine, Live2D, Blender)

The character is a rig: a fixed hierarchy of parts / bones. Blender is fully agent-drivable headless — blender -b file.blend -P script.py via the bpy API — and Blender Studio rigs document the model: an armature with IK/FK limb chains the animator poses, never regenerates.[24][25] This is the answer to the title question. Generative AI may still be used upstream (concept art, textures, background plates), but the character asset that carries the promise must live in the rig.

Why generate-and-select is not a promise, in one line: a promise is a statement about all outputs; sampling gives a statement about a distribution — and for 8 arms the distribution’s mass at exactly-8 is unknown per pose, possibly near zero for out-of-distribution anatomy, so no finite re-roll budget converts it into a guarantee. Verification converts it into a checked deliverable; construction converts it into a guarantee.

The marketing gap

Release language is qualitative and comparative — Midjourney V7’s official summary, as quoted in reference coverage, claims bodies, hands and objects have “significantly better coherence”; FLUX built its launch reputation on hands; Ideogram 3.0 on typography + “improved hands.” None publishes a number, and a dedicated cross-vendor check (Aug 2026, secondary source) concluded: “Checked against each vendor’s own documentation… None of the five publish a hand-specific accuracy figure; treat any percentage you see quoted elsewhere as unverified marketing, not a benchmark.”[16][27] Adobe’s indemnification / commercial-safety positioning for Firefly, where offered on enterprise tiers, concerns IP / legal risk of outputs, not their anatomical correctness — do not conflate the two in procurement.

Procurement implication: if a contract, SOW or product page says “exactly 8 arms,” that promise can only be underwritten by the pipeline owner — the agent / workflow builder, via construction + verification — never passed through to a model vendor. Any reseller claiming a generative model “guarantees” counts is claiming more than the model’s own maker does in its terms.

Emerging fixes, 2024–2026: real improvement, no 100%

Hand and limb quality in simple, standard poses has improved from “reliably wrong” (pre-V5, ~2022–23) to “usually right” (2025–26 flagships, ~74–90% in small independent tests). Exact counts of specified, attached, out-of-distribution parts have not crossed into reliability, let alone guarantee.

  • Midjourney V5 → V6 / V6.1 → V7 (Mar 2023 → Apr 2025, default Jun 2025): V5 was the first version to render five-fingered hands with reasonable consistency; V7’s official line is bodies / hands / objects with “significantly better coherence on all details.” Reviewer estimates put standard-pose hand success ~40% (V3) → ~85–90% (V7) — reviewer-aggregated, not vendor-published. Complex grips, interlaced fingers, multiple hands and unusual angles still fail — and there is no API.[27]
  • FLUX.1 / 1.1 / Kontext / FLUX.2 (Black Forest Labs, Aug 2024 →): launch reputation specifically on anatomy; an independent small test in this run’s sources found 86% acceptable hand anatomy (50 prompts) vs Midjourney V6.1’s 74%. Fused fingers on complex perspectives persist in the same tests; no vendor accuracy figure published.[16]
  • GPT Image line (gpt-image-1, Mar / Apr 2025 → later GPT Image models): a step-change in instruction-following, text-in-image and multi-object binding vs DALL·E 3 — but community / press hands-on of later versions still records basic failures (e.g. a four-fingered hand in GPT Image 1.5 testing, Dec 2025 — desk reviews, this run’s sources), and the numerosity literature groups GPT-4o image with FLUX / Ideogram as still failing exact counts on GeckoNum.[2]
  • Google Imagen 3 → 4 / Gemini Flash Image (“Nano Banana”) (2024 → 2025): Imagen 4 brought finer detail, 2K and better spelling — but counting is a named limitation of the flagship, in the vendor’s own words: Google DeepMind’s Imagen 4 Model Card (20 May 2025, verified against the primary source on 13 Nov 2026) lists numerical reasoning — rendering an exact number of objects — as “challenging for all models.”[41]
  • Ideogram 2 → 3 / character features (2025): typography-first gains (~85–90%+ text accuracy in reviewer testing), Style Reference, Character Reference from a single reference image. Crowd / multi-person scenes still “deform faces and fingers” (Nov 2026 desk reviews, this run’s sources); its strength is text / layout, not cardinality.
  • SD3.5 (Stability, Oct 2024): better typography and complex-prompt understanding, and open weights that enable the full ComfyUI control stack. Stability publishes no benchmark numbers for SD3.5 in its documentation, and its terms disclaim accuracy.[28]
  • Counting-aware research: CountGen 29% → 48% / 26% → 54% (roughly coin-flip, objects not limbs); noise-prior conditioning 20.0% → 85.3% and 74.8% → 86.3% (best controlled result in this run is still ~1 failure in 7, on simplified benchmarks); AIBL (arXiv preprint, NeurIPS 2026) 58–65%[39]; CountLoop “as much as 98%” — a benchmark headline for objects in a research system, not a per-image guarantee, not limbs, not a product. Its architecture is, however, the template for the verified pipeline below.

Trajectory verdict: every documented curve asymptotes below 100%, and the vendors’ own limitation lists and terms say so.

Recommended production architecture

For an 8-armed character series — the setup this report was researched for.

  1. Concept phase — generative, no promises

    ComfyUI + ControlNet + character LoRA to explore look and poses — or any API model in the tool list above for boards. Nothing here is promised to anyone.

  2. Asset phase — the promise

    Build the approved character once as an SVG / Spine / Blender rig with arm-1…arm-8. Add a CI-style check: assert arm_count == 8 on the rig file.

  3. Per-episode phase — agent-driven

    The agent poses and renders the rig headlessly. Backgrounds and props may be generative; the character may not be regenerated.

  4. If a raster generative look is non-negotiable

    ComfyUI 8-arm ControlNet + LoRA + IP-Adapter + inpaint + detector / VLM count verification on every delivered frame — and describe the deliverable honestly as “every delivered image was verified to show exactly 8 arms”: a QA claim about checked files, not a capability claim about the model.

The Bottom Line

Bottom line: Which art tool can an AI agent drive — and promise exactly 8 arms? A puppet rig: a Blender 3D armature, a Spine / Live2D 2D rig, or — simplest and most auditable — an SVG vector puppet, built with exactly eight arm parts. Eight arms in, eight arms out — in every pose, every frame, every episode. That is a promise you can put in a contract, with the rig file as the evidence. No AI image generator can make it.
