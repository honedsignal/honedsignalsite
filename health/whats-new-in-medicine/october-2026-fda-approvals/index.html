October 2026 FDA approvals, explained.

As the FDA Approval Explainer puts it: approval means that for the approved use, in the studied population, the benefits outweigh the known risks. It does not mean a cure, and it does not mean risk-free. Each entry below sticks to what the FDA announced and what the trial behind it actually showed.

Tucatinib (Tukysa) added to maintenance therapy for HER2-positive metastatic breast cancer

FDA approval — October 7, 2026

What was approved

On October 7, 2026, the FDA approved tucatinib (brand name Tukysa, from Seagen Inc., a subsidiary of Pfizer) in combination with trastuzumab and pertuzumab for the maintenance treatment of adults with unresectable locally advanced or metastatic HER2-positive breast cancer, following induction treatment.

In plain English: “maintenance” is the treatment that continues after the first round — here, 4 to 8 cycles of trastuzumab, pertuzumab, and a taxane chemotherapy — has kept the cancer from progressing. The idea is to keep it that way for longer. HER2-positive means the cancer cells carry extra copies of a growth signal called HER2; all three of these drugs target that signal. Tucatinib is a pill (300 mg, taken twice a day); the other two are given by infusion or injection.

The evidence

The approval rests on HER2CLIMB-05 (NCT05132582), a Phase 3 trial that randomly assigned 654 adults whose cancer had not progressed after induction treatment to tucatinib or a placebo, on top of trastuzumab and pertuzumab. It was double-blind — neither patients nor their doctors knew who got the real drug. Results were published in the Journal of Clinical Oncology.

The headline result, as the FDA states it: median progression-free survival — the time until the cancer grew or the patient died — was 24.9 months with tucatinib versus 16.3 months without it (hazard ratio 0.64; p < 0.0001). In absolute terms, that is an 8.6-month difference in medians. Overall survival data were not yet mature when this was analyzed, so the trial has not yet shown whether patients live longer overall — only that the cancer stayed controlled longer.

On safety, the label carries a boxed warning — the FDA's strongest — for liver toxicity (hepatotoxicity), plus warnings for diarrhea and embryo-fetal toxicity.

The grade: Promising (evidence score 12 of 14)

Who it affects

Adults whose breast cancer is HER2-positive, is locally advanced and cannot be removed by surgery or has spread to other parts of the body, and did not progress during the initial induction treatment described above. The trial included patients with and without brain metastases. Whether this treatment fits any individual situation is a question for that person's own care team — this entry only explains the approval.

Cost

The FDA approves drugs; it does not set or announce their prices, and its notice lists none. What this regimen costs a given patient will depend on pricing set by the maker and on that patient's coverage — figures not established by the approval announcement, so none are quoted here.

Atezolizumab (Tecentriq) with chemotherapy after surgery for Stage III dMMR colon cancer

FDA approval — October 8, 2026

What was approved

On October 8, 2026, the FDA approved atezolizumab (brand name Tecentriq, from Genentech, Inc.) in combination with a fluoropyrimidine and oxaliplatin — standard chemotherapy drugs — for the adjuvant treatment of adult and pediatric patients two years of age and older with Stage III mismatch repair deficient (dMMR) colon cancer. The FDA also approved the under-the-skin form, atezolizumab and hyaluronidase-tqjs (Tecentriq Hybreza), with the same chemotherapy, for patients 12 years and older who weigh at least 40 kg.

In plain English: “adjuvant” means treatment given after the main treatment — here, after surgery removed the tumor completely — to lower the chance the cancer comes back. Stage III means the cancer had reached nearby lymph nodes. dMMR means the tumor's DNA proofreading system is broken, a feature found in a minority of colon cancers; it is also the feature that makes this kind of immune-system drug (atezolizumab helps the immune system attack cancer cells) a plausible match. Eligibility is set by testing the tumor for mismatch repair status.

The evidence

The approval rests on the ATOMIC trial (NCT02912559), a Phase 3 study run at many centers that randomly assigned 712 patients (711 adults and one pediatric patient) whose Stage III dMMR colon cancer had been completely removed by surgery. Half received atezolizumab with mFOLFOX6 chemotherapy for 12 cycles, followed by atezolizumab alone for six months; half received mFOLFOX6 alone. Results were published in the New England Journal of Medicine in March 2026.

The headline result, as the FDA states it: the hazard ratio for disease-free survival was 0.50 (p = 0.0001) — the rate of cancer recurrence or death was cut in half. In the absolute numbers the headlines usually skip: at three years, 86.3% of the atezolizumab group versus 76.2% of the chemotherapy-only group were free of recurrence or death — roughly 10 more people in every 100. Median disease-free survival was not reached in either group. Overall survival did not differ significantly between the groups in the data reported so far (five-year rates of 89.7% versus 87.9%).

On safety, the label warns about immune-mediated adverse reactions — the immune system attacking healthy tissue — infusion-related reactions, complications around stem-cell transplantation, and embryo-fetal toxicity.

The grade: Promising (evidence score 11 of 14)

People — adults, and children from age two for the intravenous form — whose colon cancer was Stage III, whose tumor tests as mismatch repair deficient (dMMR), and whose tumor was completely removed by surgery. dMMR tumors are a minority of colon cancers, so this approval covers a defined subset, not colon cancer generally. Whether it fits any individual situation is a question for that person's own care team — this entry only explains the approval.
